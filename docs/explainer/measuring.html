Measuring time

Time a program once and you get a number. Time it again and you get a different one. Neither is wrong. This page explains where the difference comes from, how to turn many numbers into one honest answer, and how to say how sure you are. No formulas; the formulas are on The statistics, in detail.

In short: a timing is a sample, not the truth, so we repeat it, summarize with a number that one slow run cannot drag, say how far that summary could move, and only compare things that were measured the same way on the same machine.

The same program, different times

Run the same program twice on the same computer and the second time is a little different. Something else was using the chip. A cache held the data the first time and not the second. The chip was warmer. The operating system checked for updates. None of these are the program's fault; together they are called noise.

An analogy. Your commute is "20 minutes". Some days it is 17, some days 26, once it was 40 because of a parade. "20 minutes" is not any one day; it is what you learned from many.

Repeat, then summarize

So the study never times anything once. Each setup is run 30 times, each time from a fresh start, and the 30 numbers are boiled down to one. The 30 are a sample: thirty days of commute. The number we report is a summary of them.

Why

Why 30 and not 3? With 3 runs, one slow day changes the answer. With 30, it is one among many. More runs make the answer steadier, but each run costs minutes of machine time, and 30 was enough to make the intervals below small next to the differences the study is looking for (30 runs, 10 iterations).

Median, mean and spread

Two ways to boil 30 numbers down to one:

  • The mean: add them up, divide by 30. One parade day of 40 minutes pulls it up.
  • The median: sort them, take the middle one. The parade day is just "the largest"; it moves the median not at all.

Timings always look like the picture: a tight group and a tail of slow ones, never a tail of fast ones (nothing can run faster than the code allows). So inside one run, the study takes the median of its timed rounds. Across runs it uses an average that respects ratios, explained on Ratios, speedup and the log scale and Reading the numbers.

The spread is how far apart the 30 numbers are. Tight spread: the summary means a lot. Wide spread: be careful. The study reports the spread of every setup and looks at the ones that are wide.

How sure can we be

Thirty runs gave one summary. Thirty other runs would have given a slightly different one. The honest answer is not one number but a range: "the summary is about here, and with another thirty it would land somewhere between here and here". That range is called an interval. The narrower it is, the surer we are.

An analogy. "My commute is 20 minutes, give or take 2" says more than "20 minutes". And if your commute is 20 give or take 2, and mine is 21 give or take 2, we cannot honestly say yours is faster: the ranges overlap.

That last point is how the study decides things. Two setups are called different only when their ranges do not overlap. And "we could not tell them apart" is not the same as "they are the same": with wider ranges, a real difference can hide. Reading the numbers shows the intervals on the real data, and the next section what else has to hold before a comparison means anything.

A fair comparison

Repeating fixes noise. It does not fix bias: a difference that is there every time, for a reason that is not the thing you wanted to compare. Three rules keep the study's comparisons fair:

  • Same machine, same settings. Native, Chrome and Firefox all run on the same PC with the same cores, clock and nothing else running (The machine and its controls). A faster machine for one of them would decide the result before it was measured.
  • Mixed order. The 30 runs of every setup are shuffled together with everyone else's, so that if the machine drifts over the hours (gets warmer, say), the drift lands on all of them equally instead of on whoever went last (Random interleaved order).
  • Warm up first. The first rounds of a run are slower for reasons that have nothing to do with the code (Engines and JIT). They are run and thrown away (Warm-up inside the run).

An analogy. Comparing two routes to work means driving both in the same car, on the same weeks, not one in July and the other in a January snowstorm, and not counting the day you were still learning the way.

What to remember

In summary. One timing is a sample. Repeat 30 times, summarize with something a slow outlier cannot drag, report a range and not a point, call two things different only when the ranges do not overlap, and measure everything the same way on the same machine, in a shuffled order, after a warm-up.
