The statistics, in detail

Every statistic in analysis/, in the order the data flows through them, each with the idea, the formula, a live example on the measured/v3 data and the code. If formulas are not your thing, Reading the numbers says the same in words; this page is for checking that the words are true.

In one sentence: one median per run, a geometric mean per configuration, ratios on a log scale, bootstrap intervals over runs, and a correction for the 10 tests that decide the answers; all of it fixed in an analysis plan before the campaign ran (docs/adr/0037-analysis-plan.md, ADR 0037).

The spine of the page. Every section below is one arrow of it:

Speedup and efficiency

Strong scaling: the same 8192 x 8192 image for every N. Speedup is how many times faster N threads are than 1 thread of the same environment; efficiency is that speedup divided by N, so 1 means every thread pulled its full weight (ADR 0003; the words are introduced on Ratios, speedup and the log scale). T is the geometric mean of the 30 run values, each the median of its 10 measured iterations.

S(N) = T(1) / T(N) E(N) = S(N) / N (E = 1 is perfect)

    Geometric mean

    Why the geometric mean and not the arithmetic one, in words and with the sliders, is on An average that respects ratios. The formula:

    GM(x1..xn) = (x1 · x2 · ... · xn)^(1/n) = exp( mean( ln x1, ..., ln xn ) )
    If you already know this: the three properties the plan relied on
    • The ratio of two geometric means is the geometric mean of the ratios, so a speedup from geometric means agrees with one computed on logs, run by run (plan decision D2).
    • Times are right-skewed (a few slow runs, never a few impossibly fast ones); on a log scale they are much less so, and the geometric mean is the arithmetic mean on that scale.
    • Each run is first summarized by the median of its 10 iterations (D1), so one slow iteration barely moves it; the geometric mean then averages medians.

    Bootstrap intervals

    The idea, and the live widget where you can draw resamples from the real runs, is on How sure: the interval. Here is what the analysis does exactly: percentile bootstrap, 10 000 resamples, seed 0x5EED (24301), 95 % interval from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resampled statistics (Efron and Tibshirani 1993). No normality assumption.

    for b in 1..10000: draw 30 runs with replacement from the 30 (each group on its own) statistic_b = the statistic on that draw (a geometric mean, a ratio of them, ...) interval = [ percentile 2.5 of statistic_1..10000 , percentile 97.5 ]
    Why resample each group on its own? A speedup compares the N = 1 runs with the N = 8 runs; a RQ2 contrast compares four groups. The runs of different configurations are different processes, started at different times: there is no pairing between the 3rd run of one and the 3rd run of the other. So each group is resampled independently, and each bootstrap value is the statistic of the resampled groups (GM(resampled N = 1) / GM(resampled N = 8), say).

    A bootstrap p-value comes from the same 10 000 values: twice the share of them on the far side of "no effect" (1 for a ratio, 0 on the log scale), and never below 1/10 000, which is the resolution 10 000 resamples can give.

    The RQ2 metric

    How much does synchronization cost in one environment? Compare the atomic histogram with its lock-free control on the same input: r = ln T(atomic) - ln T(local). But r also holds things that are not synchronization, such as the dependency chain that slows the local control at k = 1. Those are the same in every environment at the same k, so compare r between environments (ADR 0026; with made-up numbers first on RQ2 with made-up numbers):

    Δr = r(browser) - r(native) exp(Δr) = [T_atomic / T_local in the browser] / [T_atomic / T_local natively] 1 = synchronization costs as much as natively; above 1 = more; below 1 = less

      Its interval resamples four groups of runs (browser atomic, browser local, native atomic, native local), each on its own. The RQ1 contrast is built the same way, with T(1) and T(N) in place of atomic and local: the browser's speedup over native's, an interaction of two ratios.

      Holm and the primary contrasts

      Why ten comparisons were named in advance and get a stricter bar is on Ten decisions, a stricter bar. The ten primary contrasts (plan decision D8): RQ2 at N = 8 for k = 1, 4, 16, 256 in both browsers (8), and RQ1 for the filter at N = 8 in both browsers (2). Everything else is exploratory: 95 % intervals, no decision.

      • Their intervals are Bonferroni 99.5 % intervals (1 - 0.05 / 10), so the ten hold together with 95 % confidence (family-wise error).
      • The decisions use Holm's step-down correction: sort the p-values, multiply the smallest by 10, the next by 9, and so on, keep a running maximum, and reject when the result is at most 0.05. It controls the same family error as Bonferroni and is never less powerful (Holm 1979).
      sort p(1) ≤ p(2) ≤ ... ≤ p(10) adjusted(i) = max over j ≤ i of min(1, (10 - j + 1) · p(j)) reject contrast i if adjusted(i) ≤ 0.05

      Mann-Whitney and Cliff's delta

      The bootstrap makes no normality assumption, but it is one method. As a robustness check, the plan repeats the 10 decisions with the course's default test for timing data: the Mann-Whitney U rank test, with Cliff's delta as its effect size.

      Why a second test at all? If two methods built on different assumptions reach the same ten decisions, the decisions do not hang on either method's assumptions. Mann-Whitney looks only at ranks (which of two runs was faster), never at how much, so it cannot be fooled by a few extreme values; the bootstrap uses the values themselves. Agreement between them is the point.

      Each run gives one ratio (log T1/TN for RQ1, log T atomic / T local for RQ2, the two cells paired by repetition index); the browser's 30 ratios are compared with native's, and the 10 p-values are Holm-adjusted.

      Cliff's δ = ( #{pairs where browser > native} - #{pairs where browser < native} ) / (30 · 30) +1 or -1 = complete separation; 0 = complete overlap

      Karp-Flatt

      Efficiency below 1 says that scaling is sub-linear, not why. Amdahl's law would blame a serial part: a fraction of the work that only one thread can do. The Karp-Flatt serial fraction (Karp and Flatt 1990) turns that around and asks which serial fraction would explain the measured speedup:

      e = (1/S - 1/N) / (1 - 1/N)

      A constant e across N reads as a serial part; an e that grows with N reads as an overhead that grows with the threads. For the filter (pick N):

      N

        Why compute it, if the filter has no serial part? Because it is the standard first suspect, and the number shows it is the wrong one: the filter's timed region has no serial part, and in weak scaling T(N) does not stay flat either. So e here is a cost of running in parallel (shared memory bandwidth natively, a fixed per-iteration overhead in the browsers, neither measured directly), not a serial fraction of the code.
