Ratios, speedup and intervals

Almost every number of the study is a ratio: one time divided by another. Speedup, efficiency, "times slower than native" and the RQ2 metric are all ratios. This page shows how to read them with made-up numbers, why they need a geometric mean and a log scale, and why a ratio of two ratios cancels what both have in common.

Times faster, times slower

Say a task takes 12 seconds one way and 4 seconds another. The second way is 3 times faster: 12 / 4 = 3. The first is 3 times slower, or "0.33 times as fast": 4 / 12 = 0.33. A ratio of exactly 1 means no difference.

The study always says which way the division goes. "Browser over native 1.3" means the browser's time divided by native's is 1.3: the browser took 1.3 times as long, 30 % longer. Below 1 would mean the browser was faster.

Speedup and efficiency

Give a job to one thread and it takes some time, call it T1. Give it to N threads and it takes TN. Speedup is how many times faster that was: T1 / TN. Efficiency is speedup divided by N: how much of each thread you actually got.

Made-up numbers: say one thread takes 80 s.

8 threads, 10 s
speedup 80 / 10 = 8, efficiency 8 / 8 = 1.0: perfect, every thread fully used
8 threads, 20 s
speedup 4, efficiency 0.5: half of every thread was wasted waiting
8 threads, 160 s
speedup 0.5: eight threads are slower than one, something is fighting

The study's filter at 8 threads has an efficiency of about 0.9; its shared-counter histogram with everyone on one counter has a speedup below 0.5. Both are real and both come back on RQ1 and RQ2.

Why

Why compare speedups and not seconds? Because the question is "does the browser scale like native?", not "is the browser as fast as native?". A browser could be 1.3 times slower at every thread count and still scale perfectly: its speedup curve would match native's exactly. The two questions are different, and the study answers both, always showing the time ratio next to the speedup (Browser over native).

Two cooks, one slower than the other. Give each of them seven helpers. "Who finishes first" is about raw speed. "Who gained the most from the helpers" is about scaling. The slow cook can win the second even while losing the first.

Why 2x and 0.5x are the same distance

Two ratios: one setup is 2 times faster (ratio 2), another is 2 times slower (ratio 0.5). They are mirror images, equally far from "equal" (ratio 1). On an ordinary number line they are not: 2 is 1 away from 1, and 0.5 is only 0.5 away. Average them the ordinary way and you get 1.25, "a bit faster", which is wrong: one doubled and one halved, that is "no change".

A log scale fixes it: each step along the line multiplies by the same amount instead of adding the same amount. On it, 0.5 and 2 are the same distance from 1, and 4 is one more step past 2. Averaging on that line gives the geometric mean, the average the study uses for every ratio and every run time. "One doubled, one halved" averages to exactly 1 there.

A pay rise of 10 % then a cut of 10 % does not bring you back where you started (you end 1 % down). Percentages compound; they live on a log scale. Ratios are the same.

So when a chart in this documentation says "log scale", read distances as "times", not "plus". The exact definition and a slider to try it are on Geometric mean.

A ratio of ratios

RQ2 asks whether synchronization (threads taking turns on shared counters) costs more in a browser than natively. The problem: a browser is slower at everything, so "the shared counters take longer in Chrome" says nothing by itself. The fix is to divide twice.

Made-up numbers. Natively, say the histogram with shared counters takes 4 s and the one with private counters (no sharing, the control) takes 1 s. Sharing cost 4 times natively. In Chrome, say they take 8 s and 2 s: sharing cost 4 times there too. Chrome is twice as slow at both, but the penalty for sharing is the same. The ratio of the two ratios is 4 / 4 = 1: no extra synchronization cost in Chrome.

Now say Chrome's shared version took 12 s instead. Sharing cost 12 / 2 = 6 times there, against 4 natively: 6 / 4 = 1.5. Synchronization costs Chrome 1.5 times what it costs natively, and that 1.5 is about synchronization alone: being a browser cancelled out, because it was in both the top and the bottom.

4 s / 1 s = 4
native: shared over control
12 s / 2 s = 6
Chrome: shared over control
6 / 4 = 1.5
the RQ2 number: Chrome's extra cost of synchronization

Two cities, two kinds of road. To ask whether city B's highways are worse relative to its streets, you compare each city's highway-to-street ratio, not its raw highway times; a city where everything is slow would otherwise look like it had bad highways.

With the real numbers, the ratio is above 1 for Chrome at small k and below 1 at large k, and about 1 or below for Firefox. RQ2: the metric, with the real numbers does this calculation on the measured data.

What to remember

In summary. A ratio says how many times. Speedup is T1 over TN, efficiency is speedup per thread, and both are about scaling, not raw speed. Ratios live on a log scale, where doubling and halving are equal steps and the right average is the geometric mean. A ratio of ratios cancels what both sides share and leaves only the thing you asked about.
