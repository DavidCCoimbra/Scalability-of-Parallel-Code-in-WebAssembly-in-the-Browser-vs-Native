Ratios, speedup and intervals

Almost every number of the study is a ratio: one time divided by another. Speedup, efficiency, "times slower than native" and the RQ2 metric are all ratios. This page shows how to read them with made-up numbers, why they need a geometric mean and a log scale, and why a ratio of two ratios cancels what both have in common.

Times faster, times slower

Say a task takes 12 seconds one way and 4 seconds another. The second way is 3 times faster: 12 / 4 = 3. The first is 3 times slower, or 0.33 times as fast: 4 / 12 = 0.33. A ratio of exactly 1 means no difference.

The study always says which way the division goes. "Browser over native 1.3" means that the browser's time divided by the native time is 1.3: the browser takes 1.3 times as long, 30% longer. A value below 1 means the browser is faster.

Speedup and efficiency

Give a job to one thread and it takes a time T1. Give it to N threads and it takes TN. Speedup is how many times faster that is: T1 / TN. Efficiency is the speedup divided by N: the share of each thread that you get.

Made-up numbers: say one thread takes 80 s.

8 threads, 10 s
speedup 80 / 10 = 8, efficiency 8 / 8 = 1.0: perfect, every thread fully used
8 threads, 20 s
speedup 4, efficiency 0.5: half of every thread was wasted waiting
8 threads, 160 s
speedup 0.5: eight threads are slower than one, something is fighting

The measured speedups and efficiencies of the study are in RQ1: scaling and RQ2: synchronization.

Why

Why compare speedups and not seconds? The question is "does the browser scale like native?", not "is the browser as fast as native?". A browser can be 1.3 times slower at every thread count and still scale perfectly: its speedup curve then matches the native one. The two questions differ, and the study answers both: it shows the time ratio next to every speedup (Browser over native).

Take two cooks, one slower than the other, and give each seven helpers. "Who finishes first" is about speed. "Who gained the most from the helpers" is about scaling. The slow cook can win the second and lose the first.

Why 2x and 0.5x are the same distance

Take two ratios: one configuration is 2 times faster (ratio 2), another is 2 times slower (ratio 0.5). They are mirror images, equally far from "equal" (ratio 1). On an ordinary number line they are not: 2 is 1 away from 1, and 0.5 is 0.5 away. Their ordinary average is 1.25, "a bit faster", which is wrong: one doubled and one halved is "no change".

A log scale fixes it: each step along the line multiplies by the same amount. On it, 0.5 and 2 are the same distance from 1, and 4 is one more step past 2. The average on that line is the geometric mean, which the study uses for every ratio and every run time. "One doubled, one halved" averages to exactly 1.

A pay rise of 10% followed by a cut of 10% does not bring you back to where you started: you end 1% down. Percentages compound, and so do ratios.

So when a chart in this documentation says "log scale", read distances as "times", not as "plus". In depth: the geometric mean has the definition and a slider to try it.

A ratio of ratios

RQ2 asks whether synchronization (threads taking turns on shared counters) costs more in a browser than natively. A browser is slower at everything, so "the shared counters take longer in Chrome" says nothing by itself. The answer is to divide twice.

With made-up numbers: natively, the histogram with shared counters takes 4 s and the one with local counters (nothing shared, the control) takes 1 s. Sharing costs 4 times natively. In Chrome, say they take 8 s and 2 s: sharing costs 4 times there too. Chrome is twice as slow at both, but the penalty for sharing is the same. The ratio of the two ratios is 4 / 4 = 1: no extra synchronization cost in Chrome.

Now say Chrome's shared version takes 12 s. Sharing costs 12 / 2 = 6 times there, against 4 natively: 6 / 4 = 1.5. Synchronization costs Chrome 1.5 times what it costs natively, and that 1.5 is about synchronization alone: "being a browser" cancels, because it is in both the top and the bottom.

native: shared over control
12 s / 2 s = 6

the RQ2 number: Chrome's extra cost of synchronization

Take two cities and two kinds of road. To ask whether city B's highways are worse relative to its streets, you compare each city's highway-to-street ratio, not the highway times. Otherwise a city where everything is slow looks like a city with bad highways.

The metric, with the real numbers does this calculation on the measured data.

What to remember

In summary. A ratio says how many times. Speedup is T1 over TN, efficiency is speedup per thread, and both are about scaling, not speed. Ratios belong on a log scale, where doubling and halving are equal steps and the right average is the geometric mean. A ratio of ratios cancels what both sides share and leaves what you asked about.
