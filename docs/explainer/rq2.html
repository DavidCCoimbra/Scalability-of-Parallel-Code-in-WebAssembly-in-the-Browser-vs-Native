RQ2: synchronization

The second question: when threads have to agree on something, does the browser pay more for that agreement than native code does? The histogram is built twice from the same pixels, once with a shared tally that every thread adds to (atomic counters), once with a private tally per piece of work merged at the end (local counters, nothing shared). The difference between the two is the price of sharing.

In one sentence: sharing costs Chrome more than it costs native code when every thread hits the same few counters, and less when the sharing is spread over many counters; Firefox never pays more than native.

The metric, with the real numbers

Two runtimes cannot be compared by the atomic histogram's time alone: the browser is already slower at everything (Browser over native). So the study asks a sharper question in three steps, here with the measured times at N = 8 and k = 1 (every pixel adds to one counter):

  1. Inside native: how many times slower is sharing than not sharing? Atomic time divided by local time, times.
  2. Inside Chrome: the same division, times.
  3. Chrome's price over native's price: . That number is the answer for this cell: 1 would mean Chrome pays for sharing exactly what native pays; 2 means twice as much.
Why a ratio of a ratio, and not just "Chrome's atomic histogram is slower"? Because it is slower at the local histogram too. If we compared atomic times directly, we would count the browser's general slowness as a cost of sharing, and conclude that sharing hurts the browser even when it does not. Dividing by each runtime's own no-sharing time first cancels that out: what remains is the price of sharing alone. See A ratio of ratios, and ADR 0026.

Now the same three steps at every N and every k. k is how many different counters the pixels are spread over: k = 1 is everyone on one counter, k = 256 is spread out (Contention).

If you already know this: the metric as the paper writes it

For each environment, k and N: r = log Tatomic - log Tlocal, the price of sharing on a log scale. Then Δr = rbrowser - rnative, and the guide shows exp(Δr), the ratio of ratios above. The log form makes the two ratios subtract, keeps 2x and 0.5x symmetric, and matches the geometric means the times are summarized with (The RQ2 metric, ADR 0026). The left chart above is on a log scale for the same reason.

Already at N = 1

Here is a clue to where Chrome's extra cost comes from. With one thread there is nobody to share with, so the price of "sharing" should be the same everywhere. It is not:

At k = 1 the atomic histogram costs Chrome times its local histogram, against times natively, with a single thread. So part of Chrome's extra cost is not about threads fighting at all: it is the atomic operation itself, which Chrome's engine turns into slower machine code. The exploratory private atomics are consistent with it with no sharing at all, and Why Chrome shows the code and the two tests that pin the cause down.

The answer to RQ2

Synchronization changes the gap, and how depends on the engine. Relative to the no-sharing control, shared atomics cost Chrome 1.4 to 2.3 times what they cost natively when the sharing is concentrated on a few counters (k = 1 to 16), and 0.42 times when it is spread over 256 counters, where Chrome's atomic histogram even scales (speedup 1.96, against native's 0.92 and Firefox's 0.97). Firefox is never worse than native: no detectable difference at k = 1, cheaper from k = 4 on.

Two things the number hides, both shown by its two factors below. Firefox's values under 1 at k = 16 and 256 do not come from cheaper atomics: its atomic times are the native ones, and its local histogram scales more than native's, which makes the denominator smaller (question 8). And Chrome's effect in both directions follows one thing, the instructions its optimizing compiler emits for the add (the probe).

What to remember. Sharing between threads is expensive everywhere. Chrome pays more than twice native's price when everyone hits the same counter, and less than half when the sharing is spread out. Firefox pays what native pays. The reason is the machine code each engine produces for the atomic add: the next page.
If you already know this: why memory order is not the explanation

WASM atomics are always sequentially consistent, which a reader may suspect as the cause. It is not: on x86 a locked read-modify-write costs about the same whatever its ordering, so Relaxed and SeqCst compile to the same instruction on these targets (Memory order, for the record). The reason is the code each engine generates around the operation.
