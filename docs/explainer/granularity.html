When parallelism pays

RQ1's answer came with a condition: "with enough work per parallel region". This page says how much is enough, in each runtime, and turns it into advice you can use. The measurements that back the advice come after it.

In one sentence: in the browser, only split work across threads when each piece is worth about half a millisecond of work; natively a third of that is enough; inside a piece, let Rayon choose the task size.

Advice for developers

Make parallel regions coarse

With 8 threads in the browser, start a parallel region only when it holds roughly 0.4 to 0.5 ms of work as one thread would do it. Below that, more than half of what the threads could give is lost to starting and stopping them. Natively about 0.12 to 0.19 ms is enough.

Let Rayon pick the task size

Inside a region, the best size for each piece a thread picks up is the same in all three runtimes, and Rayon's own choice is within 2.5% of it. Tuning it by hand for the browser gains almost nothing.

Avoid or spread synchronization

Shared atomic counters that every thread hits cost Chrome up to 2.2 times what they cost natively (RQ2). Give each thread its own counters and merge at the end, or spread the sharing over many padded counters.

Measure in the browser

One source does not give one instruction stream: the engine rewrites your atomics its own way, and that is part of the cost (Why Chrome).

Picture it. Calling eight friends to help carry boxes takes a few minutes of phone calls. For a whole house move it is nothing. For one box, you would have carried it yourself before the first call ended. The browser's phone calls take ten times longer than native's, so the house has to be bigger before calling pays.

The rest of the page is where each number above comes from: the size of a whole parallel region (the cutoff campaign), a rule of thumb from the course, and the size of each task inside a region (the grain sweep).

The cutoff campaign

The filter and the local histogram (k = 256) ran on images from 512 x 512 to 8192 x 8192 pixels, at N = 1 to 8 (4080 runs). The smallest images take a few microseconds, less than the browser's clock can tell apart, so each timed sample repeats the work several times (passes) and divides by the count (ADR 0064). Two things to see. The speedup never drops below 1: at these sizes, threads never make the work slower. But efficiency falls fast as the work shrinks, and faster in the browsers, because their fixed cost of starting and stopping the threads (fork-join) is larger.

Why "half the threads' worth" as the line? Some line is needed to turn a curve into one number, and efficiency 0.5 (8 threads doing the work of 4) is the point where the overhead has eaten as much as it left. The study reads where each curve crosses it, and reports the work per call at that point:

For the filter that is 123 µs of single-thread work natively, 399 in Chrome and 469 in Firefox: the browsers need 3 to 4 times the work native needs to keep half the threads' worth. That is the "0.4 to 0.5 ms" of the advice. In Firefox it is about the cost of 8 fork-joins (8 × 60 µs); natively it is 1.4 times that (8 × 10.8 µs).

The 100:1 rule

A rule of thumb from the course: a parallel region should do at least 100 times the work its fork-join costs, so that the overhead stays under 1%. It gives a second, stricter line than efficiency 0.5. With the fork-join this campaign measured (300 runs per environment), applied to the full 8192 x 8192 image at N = 8:

Only native keeps the fork-join under 1% of the time at 8192² (0.33%; Firefox 1.45%, Chrome 2.7%). To reach the 1% line, the browsers need 6 to 10 times more work per region than native (last column), because their fork-join costs that many times more.

Two lines, two numbers. "3 to 4 times the work" and "6 to 10 times the work" measure the same thing, the browser's dearer fork-join, against two different lines. Efficiency 0.5 is a loose line (lose half); the 100:1 rule is a strict one (lose 1%). The browser's handicap looks bigger against the strict line because its fork-join is a bigger share of a smaller budget. Both say: in the browser, make the regions bigger.

The grain U-curve

Inside one region, Rayon cuts the work into tasks that idle threads pick up (Grain size). Cut too small, and the cost of handling each task dominates; cut too large, and there are too few tasks to keep all the threads busy. Somewhere in between is a flat bottom: the U-curve. The grain sweep (4050 runs, N = 8) fixes the task size by hand and measures the time; adaptive is Rayon's own split.

Workload:Image:

  • At 8192² the best task is 4 rows for the filter and 64 to 128 KB for the local histogram, in all three runtimes. The bottom of the U is in the same place.
  • Too fine: 1 KB histogram tasks cost 2.1 to 3.0 times the best. Too coarse: one task per thread, with nothing left for an idle thread to pick up, costs 1.01 times the best natively and 1.13 to 1.16 in the browsers.
  • Rayon's adaptive split is within 2.5% of the best. Hence the advice: let it choose.
If you already know this: the best grains, and the 100:1 rule applied per task

The 100:1 rule lands in or near the flat region of best grains when it is applied to the cost per task (0.46 to 1.0 µs for the histogram, last columns), not to the region's fork-join: the per-task overhead is what a too-fine grain pays for, so that is the cost the rule should be set against inside a region.

What to remember. Threads in the browser pay off the same way as native ones, but the entry ticket is 3 to 10 times bigger. Make each parallel region worth about half a millisecond of work, and let Rayon cut it up.
