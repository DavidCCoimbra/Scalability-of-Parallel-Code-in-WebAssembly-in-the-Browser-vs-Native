How a study can be wrong

A careful study can still be wrong in four ways, which researchers call threats to validity: something else caused the effect, the measure does not capture the question, the result holds only here, or the numbers do not support the conclusion. This page explains each with an example. The study's own answers are in Threats to validity.

Something else caused it

You change one thing and see a difference. Was it the thing you changed? If anything else changed at the same time, the difference may come from that. This is internal validity: whether the cause is the cause.

A café changes its coffee recipe and sales go up. The same week a new barista started and the rain stopped. Was it the recipe?

In this study: if Chrome ran on other cores than native, or with turbo on, or after the machine had warmed up, a difference between them could be the cores, the clock or the heat, not the browser. That is why everything runs on the same 8 cores, at a fixed clock, in a shuffled order (A fair comparison). The study also found a case of exactly this and fixed it: Chrome was using cores outside its mask (A browser that left the mask).

The wrong thing was measured

You want to know X, and you measure Y because Y is easy to measure. Does Y stand for X? This is construct validity: whether the number stands for the idea.

You want to know whether a restaurant is good, so you count its online likes. Likes measure popularity and marketing; they may or may not measure the food.

In this study: "does the browser scale like native" is measured by comparing speedup curves. A speedup flatters a slow start (an environment that is slow at 1 thread has more room to gain), so the study shows the time ratio next to it (Speedup and efficiency). And "the cost of synchronization" is a ratio of ratios, so that the browser's general slowness does not count as a synchronization cost (A ratio of ratios).

It only holds here

The result is real on this machine, with this program and these versions. Does it hold on another? This is external validity: how far the result carries.

A survey of one city's commuters says that most people cycle. That is true there. It says nothing about the next city, where it rains all year.

In this study: one PC, one chip, two browser versions, two workloads, one image size. Everything that makes the comparison exact on this machine also makes it a single case. The study says so, lists what it tried beyond that (other sizes, other builds, the E-cores), and claims no more (External).

The numbers do not support it

The comparison is fair and the right thing is measured, but the difference is small and the measurements are few. Could it be chance? This is conclusion validity: whether the numbers support the claim.

Three customers prefer the new recipe and two the old. "Customers prefer the new recipe" is not a conclusion; it is five people.

In this study: every reported number comes from 30 runs and has a range around it, and a difference is claimed only when the ranges do not overlap. Ten comparisons were named in advance as the ones that answer the questions, and they get a stricter bar, because many questions at once make it easier for one to look different by chance (Ten decisions, a stricter bar). And "no difference found" is reported as that, never as "they are the same" (Not rejecting is not proof).

What to remember

In summary. Four questions: was it the cause, was it the right thing, does it hold beyond here, do the numbers support it. A careful study answers all four, including the ones where the answer is "only partly".
