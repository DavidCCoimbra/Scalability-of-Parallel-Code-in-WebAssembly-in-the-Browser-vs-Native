Threats to validity

A result can be wrong in ways its numbers do not show. This page lists how the study could be wrong, what controls each threat, and what is left: one machine, a possible crowding of the cores at 8 threads, explanations supported to different degrees, and intervals that bound what "no difference" can mean.

The four kinds of threat below are explained with an example each in Four ways a study fools itself.

The three that matter most

Three things are left open, and one limit applies to every "no difference" in the study:

Only one machine

Every number comes from one PC, one CPU, one operating system. Another CPU, especially one whose cores share cache lines differently, could move the RQ2 numbers. The study says what the machine is and does not claim more.

Crowded cores at N = 8

With 8 threads on 8 cores, the browser's own threads and the worker that coordinates the run have nowhere else to go. How much they take during a timed iteration was not established, so the N = 8 results of the browsers may include it.

Explanations at different levels

Why Chrome pays more for a shared counter was tested by changing one thing, the instructions of the add, and the effect followed. Why the same instructions win at k = 256 is a proposed mechanism. Why the filter scales as it does is supported by measurements that agree, not proven (What explains the results).

And one limit: "no difference" is not "the same"

When a comparison finds no difference, it only means the difference is smaller than the interval could see: the widest intervals are shown below. A real difference smaller than that would pass unnoticed.

Internal

Could something other than the runtime explain the differences? Other programs using the cores, the browser starting up, a drifting clock, a different browser version: anything that changes the time but is not what the study compares.

Construct

Do the numbers measure what the questions ask? A speedup that hides who is faster in seconds, a metric that counts the wrong cost, a counter read as a cause: ways of measuring something real but not the thing asked.

External

How far do the results carry beyond this setup? One machine, two browser versions, two workloads, one image size. The study was built for a fair comparison, not for a typical desktop.

Conclusion

Do the numbers support what is concluded from them? Enough runs, the right unit of analysis, a bar raised for testing ten things at once, and "not rejected" never read as "equal" (Not rejecting is not proof).

In summary. The results are solid for this machine and this setup, came back the same when everything was measured again, and are honest about their limits: one PC, a possible crowding at N = 8, explanations that are tested, supported or open and labelled as such, and a few percent of difference that the intervals cannot see.
