Threats to validity

Every measurement can be wrong in ways its numbers do not show. A careful study ends by listing how it could be wrong, what was done about each, and what is left. This page does that for this study, in plain words first and then in full.

In one sentence: the known ways to be wrong are controlled or measured; what is left is one machine, a possible crowding of the cores at N = 8 in Firefox, an explanation of Chrome that was observed but not tested by changing something, and intervals that bound what "no difference" can mean.

The four kinds of threat below are explained with an example each in Four ways a study fools itself.

The three that matter most

Three threats are left open, and one limit applies to every "no difference" in the study:

Only one machine

Every number comes from one PC, one CPU, one operating system. Another CPU, especially one whose cores share cache lines differently, could move the RQ2 numbers. The study says what the machine is and does not claim more.

Crowded cores at N = 8

With 8 threads on 8 cores, the browser's own threads and the worker that coordinates the run have nowhere else to go. A pilot was consistent with this costing Firefox something at N = 8, but could not separate it from other effects, so Firefox's N = 8 results may include it.

An explanation observed, not tested

The counters show Chrome's loop retrying and reading the line first. Nobody changed the loop and measured again, so the counters show the mechanism, not proof that it is the whole cause. And why Chrome wins at k = 256 is not explained at all.

And one limit: "no difference" is not "the same"

When a comparison finds no difference, it only means the difference is smaller than the interval could see: within about ±4% for RQ1 and ±6.5% for RQ2. A 3% real difference would pass unnoticed.

Internal

Could something other than the runtime explain the differences? Other programs using the cores, the browser starting up, a drifting clock, a different browser version: anything that changes the time but is not what the study compares.

Construct

Do the numbers measure what the questions ask? A speedup that hides who is faster in seconds, a metric that counts the wrong cost, a counter read as a cause: ways of measuring something real but not the thing asked.

External

How far do the results carry beyond this setup? One machine, two browser versions, two workloads, one image size. The study was built for a fair comparison, not for a typical desktop.

Conclusion

Do the numbers support what is concluded from them? Enough runs, the right unit of analysis, a bar raised for testing ten things at once, and "not rejected" never read as "equal" (Not rejecting is not proof).
