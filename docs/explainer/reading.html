Analysis plan and statistics

Thirty runs give thirty slightly different times. The study reports one time with an interval around it, and answers ten questions with yes or no. This page explains how, first in words (one value per run, an average that respects ratios, the interval, the ten decisions) and then statistic by statistic with the formula, a live example on the measured data and the code.

One value per run

10 measured iterations become 1 number: the median.

One time per configuration

30 run values become 1 time: an average made for ratios.

A range around it

Resample the 30 runs many times to see how far the time could move.

Ten decisions

The ten primary comparisons get a stricter bar, so they hold together.

One value per run

Each run times the workload 10 times. Those 10 are not independent measurements: they share everything that the program start decided (The run is the unit). So the analysis first reduces them to one number per run, the median: sort the 10 times and take the middle.

Why

Why the median, and not the mean of the 10? One odd iteration should not move the run. If an interrupt makes one iteration take twice as long, the mean of the 10 rises by 10%, and the median does not move. Median, mean and spread explains both terms.

From here on every statistic works on 30 numbers per configuration, one per run. The iterations are not used again.

An average that respects ratios

The 30 run values now become one time for the configuration. The study does not use the arithmetic mean (add and divide by 30). It uses the geometric mean, because every answer of the study is a ratio: "8 threads are 7 times faster", "Chrome takes 1.2 times the native time". Ratios have a symmetry that the arithmetic mean breaks.

Say one run is twice as slow as expected (ratio 2) and another twice as fast (ratio 0.5). The two cancel: on average, no change. The arithmetic mean says (2 + 0.5) / 2 = 1.25, a 25% slowdown. The geometric mean says 1. Move the two ratios below: only the geometric mean gives "no change" when the two cancel.

The sliders are free inputs, not data. The axis is a log scale: equal distances are equal ratios, so 0.5 and 2 are at the same distance from 1 (Why 2x and 0.5x are the same distance).

Why

Why does this matter for times, which are not ratios? For two reasons. The times appear in the answers only as ratios, and with the geometric mean the answer is the same whether you divide first or average first; with the arithmetic mean it is not. And run times are skewed: a run can be much slower than usual, never much faster. The slow tail pulls the arithmetic mean much more than the geometric one. The analysis plan fixed this choice before the campaign ran (ADR 0037).

So one configuration has one time: the geometric mean of its 30 run values. The speedup at 8 threads is that time at N = 1 divided by that time at N = 8 (Speedup and efficiency).

How sure: the interval

There are 30 runs. With 30 other runs, the geometric mean would be a little different. The interval says how different: the range in which the time would likely fall if the campaign were taken again. A narrow interval means that 30 runs are plenty; a wide one means that the configuration is noisy.

Why

How do you know what 30 more runs would give, without taking them? With the bootstrap. Treat the 30 runs as the whole population. Draw 30 of them at random with replacement (some are drawn twice, some not at all) and compute the geometric mean of that resample. Repeat 10 000 times. Drop the lowest 2.5% and the highest 2.5% of the 10 000 means: what remains is the 95% interval. The method assumes nothing about the shape of the data, which matters because run times are skewed (Efron and Tibshirani 1993).

Half-width. Half the width of the interval, as a percentage of the estimate. "±4%" means that the true value is likely within 4% of the number given. The primary comparisons have half-widths of a few percent (30 runs, 10 iterations).

Ten decisions, a stricter bar

Most of the study describes: a speedup and its interval. Ten comparisons are its answers, and each gets a yes or a no: "Chrome's filter scales as native does", "synchronization costs Chrome more than native at k = 1". The ten were named before any data existed: RQ2 at N = 8 for the four values of k in both browsers (8), and RQ1 for the filter at N = 8 in both browsers (2). They are the primary contrasts. Everything else is exploratory: reported with its interval and no decision.

Why

Why name them in advance? With 162 configurations you always find something that looks like a difference, by chance alone. To look at everything and then report the striking ones as findings is to report the chance. Naming the ten before the campaign (the analysis plan, ADR 0037) means that the data cannot choose its own test.

Why

Why a stricter bar for them? A 95% interval misses the true value about one time in twenty. Ask ten questions that way, and the chance that at least one answer is wrong rises to about 40%. So the ten get a bar that keeps the whole set at 95%: each gets a 99.5% interval (Bonferroni: the 5% divided by ten), and the decisions use Holm's rule, which gives the same guarantee and is less strict on the clearest cases. The smallest p-value must pass the highest bar, the next one a slightly lower bar, and so on.

Eight of the ten pass the bar. The next section is about the two that do not: Chrome's filter and Firefox at k = 1.

Not rejecting is not proof

Firefox's synchronization cost at k = 1 is 0.99 times the native one, with an interval from about 0.94 to 1.06. It does not pass the bar: the study cannot say that Firefox differs from native there. That is not "Firefox equals native". It is "the difference, if there is one, lies between 6% less and 6% more, and the measurement cannot tell which". The same holds for Chrome's filter speedup: 0.99 times the native one, with an interval from about 0.95 to 1.03.

Compare a kitchen scale that reads to the nearest 50 g. Two parcels that both read 1 kg are not shown to weigh the same; they are shown to differ by less than the scale resolves. The interval is the resolution of the measurement, and more runs make it finer.

Why

Why state it this carefully? "No detectable difference" and "the same" lead to different conclusions. The paper says the first, bounded by the interval: Chrome's filter and Firefox at k = 1 are "within the interval of native", never "equal to native". A reader who wants a tighter bound knows what it takes: more runs.

    Why

    Why is a speedup never read alone? A speedup hides a slow start: a browser that is slow at N = 1 has more room to gain, and can seem to scale better than native while it is slower at every N. So every speedup is read next to the time ratio browser / native at the same N (ADR 0039), which is in Browser over native.

    In depth: The code: speedup with its bootstrap interval

    In depth: the geometric mean

    The formula:

    GM(x1..xn) = (x1 · x2 · ... · xn)^(1/n) = exp( mean( ln x1, ..., ln xn ) )
    In depth: the three properties the plan relied on
    • The ratio of two geometric means is the geometric mean of the ratios, so a speedup from geometric means agrees with one computed on logs, run by run (plan decision D2).
    • Times are right-skewed (a few slow runs, never a few impossibly fast ones); on a log scale they are much less so, and the geometric mean is the arithmetic mean on that scale.
    • Each run is first summarized by the median of its 10 iterations (D1), so one slow iteration barely moves it; the geometric mean then averages medians.

    In depth: bootstrap intervals

    The analysis uses the percentile bootstrap: 10 000 resamples, seed 0x5EED (24301), and a 95% interval from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resampled statistics (Efron and Tibshirani 1993).

    for b in 1..10000: draw 30 runs with replacement from the 30 (each group on its own) statistic_b = the statistic on that draw (a geometric mean, a ratio of them, ...) interval = [ percentile 2.5 of statistic_1..10000 , percentile 97.5 ]

    Why

    Why resample each group on its own? A speedup compares the N = 1 runs with the N = 8 runs; an RQ2 contrast compares four groups. The runs of different configurations are different processes, started at different times: nothing pairs the 3rd run of one with the 3rd run of another. So each group is resampled independently, and each bootstrap value is the statistic of the resampled groups, for example GM(resampled N = 1) / GM(resampled N = 8).

    A bootstrap p-value comes from the same 10 000 values: twice the share of them beyond "no effect" (1 for a ratio, 0 on the log scale). It is never below 1/10 000, the resolution of 10 000 resamples.

    In depth: The code: one resample, and the percentile interval

    In depth: the RQ2 metric

    In one environment the cost of synchronization is r = ln T(atomic) - ln T(local), on the same input. But r also holds things that are not synchronization, such as the dependency chain that slows the local histogram at k = 1. Those are the same in every environment at the same k, so r is compared between environments (ADR 0026; RQ2 with made-up numbers shows the arithmetic):

    Δr = r(browser) - r(native) exp(Δr) = [T_atomic / T_local in the browser] / [T_atomic / T_local natively] 1 = synchronization costs as much as natively; above 1 = more; below 1 = less

      In depth: the interaction on logs, and the RQ1 contrast

      Its interval resamples four groups of runs (browser atomic, browser local, native atomic, native local), each on its own. The RQ1 contrast has the same form, with T(1) and T(N) in place of atomic and local: the speedup of the browser over the native speedup, an interaction of two ratios.

      In depth: Holm and the primary contrasts

      The ten primary contrasts are plan decision D8. Exploratory comparisons get 95% intervals and no decision.

      • The intervals of the ten are Bonferroni intervals of 99.5% (1 - 0.05 / 10), so the ten hold together at 95% (family-wise error).
      • The decisions use Holm's step-down correction: sort the p-values, multiply the smallest by 10, the next by 9, and so on, keep a running maximum, and reject when the result is at most 0.05. It controls the same family-wise error as Bonferroni and is never less powerful (Holm 1979).
      sort p(1) ≤ p(2) ≤ ... ≤ p(10) adjusted(i) = max over j ≤ i of min(1, (10 - j + 1) · p(j)) reject contrast i if adjusted(i) ≤ 0.05

      In depth: Mann-Whitney and Cliff's delta

      The bootstrap is one method. As a robustness check, the plan repeats the 10 decisions with the course's default test for timing data: the Mann-Whitney U rank test, with Cliff's delta as its effect size.

      Why

      Why a second test? If two methods with different assumptions reach the same ten decisions, the decisions do not depend on the assumptions of either. Mann-Whitney uses only ranks (which of two runs is faster), never by how much, so a few extreme values cannot move it; the bootstrap uses the values themselves.

      Each run gives one ratio: log T1/TN for RQ1, log T atomic / T local for RQ2, with the two configurations paired by repetition index. The 30 ratios of the browser are compared with the 30 native ones, and the 10 p-values are Holm-adjusted.

      Cliff's δ = ( #{pairs where browser > native} - #{pairs where browser < native} ) / (30 · 30) +1 or -1 = complete separation; 0 = complete overlap

      In depth: Karp-Flatt

      An efficiency below 1 says that scaling is sub-linear, not why. Amdahl's law attributes it to a serial part: a fraction of the work that only one thread can do. The Karp-Flatt serial fraction (Karp and Flatt 1990) asks the reverse: which serial fraction would explain the measured speedup?

      e = (1/S - 1/N) / (1 - 1/N)

      A constant e across N reads as a serial part; an e that grows with N reads as an overhead that grows with the threads. For the filter (pick N):

      N

        Why

        Why compute it, if the filter has no serial part? A serial part is the standard first suspect, and the number shows that it is the wrong one: the timed region of the filter has no serial part, and in weak scaling T(N) does not stay flat either. So e here is a cost of running in parallel (shared memory bandwidth natively, a fixed overhead per iteration in the browsers, neither measured directly), not a serial fraction of the code (Fork-join in the results).

        In summary. One value per run (the median of its 10 measured iterations), one time per configuration (the geometric mean of 30 runs), a percentile bootstrap interval over runs (10 000 resamples, fixed seed), Bonferroni intervals and Holm decisions for the ten primary contrasts, and Mann-Whitney as a second test. The analysis plan fixed all of it before the campaign (ADR 0037). Not rejecting means "could not tell", never "the same".
