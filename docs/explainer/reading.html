Reading the numbers

Thirty runs give thirty slightly different times. The paper reports one time, with a range around it, and says "yes" or "no" to ten questions. This page is how we get from the thirty to the one, in plain words and with no formulas. The formulas, for anyone who wants them, are on The statistics, in detail.

In one sentence: each run becomes one number; the 30 numbers become one time, averaged in a way that treats "twice as fast" and "twice as slow" fairly; a range around it says how sure we are; and the ten answers that matter are held to a stricter bar than everything else.

One value per run

Each run times the workload 10 times. Those 10 are not independent measurements, as The run is the unit explains: they share everything the program start decided. So the first thing the analysis does is collapse them into one number per run. Which one? The median: sort the 10 times, take the one in the middle.

Why the middle one, and not the average of the 10? Because one odd iteration should not drag the run. If a background interrupt makes one iteration take twice as long, the average of the 10 moves up by 10 %, but the median does not move at all: the middle value is still in the middle. The median is the honest "typical iteration" of that run. (If the words median and mean are new, the page Median, mean and spread has them with pictures.)

From here on, every statistic works on 30 numbers per configuration, one per run, and the iterations are never looked at again.

An average that respects ratios

Now 30 run values have to become one time for the configuration. The usual average, add them up and divide by 30, is not the one used. The study uses the geometric mean, and the reason is that every answer in the study is a ratio: "8 threads are 7 times faster", "Chrome takes 1.2 times native's time". Ratios have a symmetry that the usual average breaks.

Picture it. One run came out twice as slow as expected (ratio 2) and another twice as fast (ratio 0.5). Common sense says these two cancel: on average, no change. The usual average says (2 + 0.5) / 2 = 1.25, a 25 % slowdown. The geometric mean says 1, no change. Move the two ratios below and watch both averages: only the geometric one lands on "no change" when the two cancel.

The sliders are free inputs, not data. The axis is a log scale: equal distances are equal ratios, so 0.5 and 2 sit at the same distance from 1 (Why 2x and 0.5x are the same distance).

Why does this matter for times, which are not ratios? Two reasons. First, the times only ever appear in the answers as ratios, and the ratio of two geometric means is the geometric mean of the ratios, so the answer does not depend on whether you divide first or average first. With the usual average it would. Second, run times are lopsided: a run can be much slower than typical (a hiccup), never much faster than typical. The usual average is pulled toward the slow tail; the geometric mean much less. The plan fixed this choice before the campaign ran (ADR 0037).

So: one configuration, one time, the geometric mean of its 30 run values. The speedup of 8 threads is that time at N = 1 divided by that time at N = 8 (Speedup and efficiency).

How sure: the interval

We have 30 runs. If we took 30 more, the geometric mean would come out a little different. How different? That is what the interval says: the range in which the time would most probably land if the whole thing were done again. A narrow interval means 30 runs were plenty; a wide one means the configuration is noisy and the one number should be held loosely.

How can we know what 30 more runs would give, without taking them? With a trick called the bootstrap. Pretend the 30 runs we have are the whole world. Draw 30 of them at random, with replacement (so some get picked twice and some not at all), and compute the geometric mean of that pretend sample. Do it again. And again, 10 000 times. The 10 000 pretend means spread out; throw away the lowest 2.5 % and the highest 2.5 %, and what is left is the 95 % interval. It assumes nothing about the shape of the data, which matters because run times are lopsided (Efron and Tibshirani 1993).

Notice how much narrower the pile is than the spread of the dots. The dots are single runs; the pile is averages of 30. Averages of 30 wobble far less than single runs, and the interval measures that wobble. For the filter, the pile is a sliver: the time is known to within about 1 %. For the atomic histogram at k = 1 it is wider, because single runs of that configuration differ a lot.

Half-width. Half the width of the interval, as a percentage of the estimate. "±4 %" means the true value is probably within 4 % above or below the number given. The study's main answers have half-widths of a few percent (30 runs, 10 iterations).

Ten decisions, a stricter bar

Most of the study describes: here is a speedup, here is its interval. But ten comparisons are the study's actual answers, and for each the paper says yes or no: "Chrome's filter scales like native" (yes or no), "synchronization costs Chrome more than native at k = 1" (yes or no). Those ten were named before any data existed: RQ2 at N = 8 for the four values of k in both browsers (8), and RQ1 for the filter at N = 8 in both browsers (2). They are the primary contrasts; everything else is exploratory, reported with its interval and no yes-or-no.

Why name them in advance? Because with 162 configurations you can always find something that looks like a difference, by luck alone. If we looked at everything and then picked the striking ones to report as findings, we would be reporting the luck. Naming the ten before the campaign (the analysis plan, ADR 0037) means the data could not choose its own test.
Why a stricter bar for them? A 95 % interval is wrong about 5 % of the time: one time in twenty, the true value is outside it. Ask ten questions that way and the chance that at least one of the ten answers is a fluke climbs to about 40 %. That is too high for the answers the paper stands on. So the ten get a bar that keeps the whole set at 95 %: each gets a 99.5 % interval (Bonferroni: 5 % shared ten ways), and the yes-or-no decisions use a slightly smarter rule, Holm's, that gives the same guarantee without being needlessly strict on the clearest cases. In words: the smallest surprise has to clear the highest bar, the next one a slightly lower bar, and so on down.

Not rejecting is not proof

Firefox's synchronization cost at k = 1 came out at 0.99 times native's, with an interval from about 0.94 to 1.06. The bar was not cleared: we cannot say Firefox differs from native there. It is tempting to read that as "Firefox equals native". It does not say that. It says "the difference, if any, is somewhere between 6 % cheaper and 6 % more expensive, and we could not tell which". The same holds for Chrome's filter speedup, 0.99 times native's with an interval from about 0.95 to 1.03.

Picture it. You weigh two parcels on a kitchen scale that reads to the nearest 50 g, and both read 1 kg. You have not shown they weigh the same; you have shown they differ by less than the scale can see. The interval is the scale's resolution. With more runs, the scale gets finer.
Why say it this carefully? Because "no detectable difference" and "the same" lead to different conclusions. The paper says the first, bounded by the interval: the Firefox filter and Firefox's k = 1 cell are "within the interval of native", never "equal to native". Any reader who wants a tighter bound knows exactly what it would take: more runs.
What to remember. One number per run (the median of 10), one time per configuration (the geometric mean of 30), one interval per time (10 000 pretend re-draws of the 30), and ten yes-or-no answers held to a bar that keeps all ten right 95 % of the time. A "no" means "could not tell", never "the same".
