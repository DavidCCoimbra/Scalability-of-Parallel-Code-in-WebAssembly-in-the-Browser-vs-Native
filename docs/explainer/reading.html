Analysis plan and statistics

Thirty runs give thirty slightly different times. The study reports one time with an interval around it, and answers ten questions with yes or no. This page explains how, first in words (one value per run, an average that respects ratios, the interval, the ten decisions) and then statistic by statistic with the formula, a live example on the measured data and the code.

One value per run

Each run times the workload 10 times. Those 10 are not independent measurements, as The run is the unit explains: they share everything the program start decided. So the first thing the analysis does is collapse them into one number per run. Which one? The median: sort the 10 times, take the one in the middle.

Why

Why the middle one, and not the average of the 10? Because one odd iteration should not drag the run. If a background interrupt makes one iteration take twice as long, the average of the 10 moves up by 10 %, but the median does not move at all: the middle value is still in the middle. The median is the honest "typical iteration" of that run. (If the words median and mean are new, the page Median, mean and spread has them with pictures.)

From here on, every statistic works on 30 numbers per configuration, one per run, and the iterations are never looked at again.

An average that respects ratios

Now 30 run values have to become one time for the configuration. The usual average, add them up and divide by 30, is not the one used. The study uses the geometric mean, and the reason is that every answer in the study is a ratio: "8 threads are 7 times faster", "Chrome takes 1.2 times native's time". Ratios have a symmetry that the usual average breaks.

One run came out twice as slow as expected (ratio 2) and another twice as fast (ratio 0.5). Common sense says these two cancel: on average, no change. The usual average says (2 + 0.5) / 2 = 1.25, a 25 % slowdown. The geometric mean says 1, no change. Move the two ratios below and watch both averages: only the geometric one lands on "no change" when the two cancel.

The sliders are free inputs, not data. The axis is a log scale: equal distances are equal ratios, so 0.5 and 2 sit at the same distance from 1 (Why 2x and 0.5x are the same distance).

Why

Why does this matter for times, which are not ratios? Two reasons. First, the times only ever appear in the answers as ratios, and the ratio of two geometric means is the geometric mean of the ratios, so the answer does not depend on whether you divide first or average first. With the usual average it would. Second, run times are lopsided: a run can be much slower than typical (a hiccup), never much faster than typical. The usual average is pulled toward the slow tail; the geometric mean much less. The plan fixed this choice before the campaign ran (ADR 0037).

So: one configuration, one time, the geometric mean of its 30 run values. The speedup of 8 threads is that time at N = 1 divided by that time at N = 8 (Speedup and efficiency).

How sure: the interval

There are 30 runs. With 30 more, the geometric mean would come out a little different. How different? That is what the interval says: the range in which the time would most probably land if the whole thing were done again. A narrow interval means 30 runs were plenty; a wide one means the configuration is noisy and the one number should be held loosely.

Why

How do you know what 30 more runs would give, without taking them? With a trick called the bootstrap. Pretend the 30 runs are the whole world. Draw 30 of them at random, with replacement (so some get picked twice and some not at all), and compute the geometric mean of that pretend sample. Do it again. And again, 10 000 times. The 10 000 pretend means spread out; throw away the lowest 2.5 % and the highest 2.5 %, and what is left is the 95 % interval. It assumes nothing about the shape of the data, which matters because run times are lopsided (Efron and Tibshirani 1993).

Notice how much narrower the pile is than the spread of the dots. The dots are single runs; the pile is averages of 30. Averages of 30 wobble far less than single runs, and the interval measures that wobble. For the filter, the pile is a sliver: the time is known to within about 1 %. For the atomic histogram at k = 1 it is wider, because single runs of that configuration differ a lot.

Half-width. Half the width of the interval, as a percentage of the estimate. "±4 %" means the true value is probably within 4 % above or below the number given. The study's main answers have half-widths of a few percent (30 runs, 10 iterations).

Ten decisions, a stricter bar

Most of the study describes: here is a speedup, here is its interval. But ten comparisons are the study's actual answers, and for each the study says yes or no: "Chrome's filter scales like native" (yes or no), "synchronization costs Chrome more than native at k = 1" (yes or no). Those ten were named before any data existed: RQ2 at N = 8 for the four values of k in both browsers (8), and RQ1 for the filter at N = 8 in both browsers (2). They are the primary contrasts; everything else is exploratory, reported with its interval and no yes-or-no.

Why

Why name them in advance? Because with 162 configurations you can always find something that looks like a difference, by luck alone. Looking at everything and then picking the striking ones to report as findings would report the luck. Naming the ten before the campaign (the analysis plan, ADR 0037) means the data could not choose its own test.

Why

Why a stricter bar for them? A 95 % interval is wrong about 5 % of the time: one time in twenty, the true value is outside it. Ask ten questions that way and the chance that at least one of the ten answers is a fluke climbs to about 40 %. That is too high for the answers the paper stands on. So the ten get a bar that keeps the whole set at 95 %: each gets a 99.5 % interval (Bonferroni: 5 % shared ten ways), and the yes-or-no decisions use a slightly smarter rule, Holm's, that gives the same guarantee without being needlessly strict on the clearest cases. In words: the smallest surprise has to clear the highest bar, the next one a slightly lower bar, and so on down.

Not rejecting is not proof

Firefox's synchronization cost at k = 1 came out at 0.99 times native's, with an interval from about 0.94 to 1.06. The bar was not cleared: the study cannot say that Firefox differs from native there. It is tempting to read that as "Firefox equals native". It does not say that. It says "the difference, if any, is somewhere between 6 % cheaper and 6 % more expensive, and the measurement cannot tell which". The same holds for Chrome's filter speedup, 0.99 times native's with an interval from about 0.95 to 1.03.

You weigh two parcels on a kitchen scale that reads to the nearest 50 g, and both read 1 kg. You have not shown they weigh the same; you have shown they differ by less than the scale can see. The interval is the scale's resolution. With more runs, the scale gets finer.

Why

Why say it this carefully? Because "no detectable difference" and "the same" lead to different conclusions. The paper says the first, bounded by the interval: the Firefox filter and Firefox's k = 1 cell are "within the interval of native", never "equal to native". Any reader who wants a tighter bound knows exactly what it would take: more runs.

In depth: speedup and efficiency

The rest of this page gives each statistic of analysis/ in the order the data flows through them. Every section below is one arrow of this chain:

10 measured iterations --median--> 1 value per run (1) 30 run values --geometric mean--> T per configuration (2) --> speedup, efficiency, ratios (1, 4, 7) 30 run values --resample 10 000x--> intervals, p-values (3) 10 primary p-values --Holm--> 10 decisions (5) the same 10, by ranks --Mann-Whitney--> the decisions re-checked (6)

Strong scaling: the same 8192 x 8192 image for every N. Speedup is how many times faster N threads are than 1 thread of the same environment; efficiency is that speedup divided by N, so 1 means every thread pulled its full weight (ADR 0003; the words are introduced on Ratios, speedup and intervals). T is the geometric mean of the 30 run values, each the median of its 10 measured iterations.

S(N) = T(1) / T(N) E(N) = S(N) / N (E = 1 is perfect)

    Why

    Why is speedup never read alone? A speedup hides a slow start: a browser that is slow at N = 1 has more room to gain and can look as if it scales better than native while being slower at every N. So every speedup is read next to the absolute time ratio browser / native at the same N (ADR 0039), shown on Browser over native.

    In depth: The code: speedup with its bootstrap interval

    In depth: the geometric mean

    Why the geometric mean and not the arithmetic one, in words and with the sliders, is on An average that respects ratios. The formula:

    GM(x1..xn) = (x1 · x2 · ... · xn)^(1/n) = exp( mean( ln x1, ..., ln xn ) )
    In depth: the three properties the plan relied on
    • The ratio of two geometric means is the geometric mean of the ratios, so a speedup from geometric means agrees with one computed on logs, run by run (plan decision D2).
    • Times are right-skewed (a few slow runs, never a few impossibly fast ones); on a log scale they are much less so, and the geometric mean is the arithmetic mean on that scale.
    • Each run is first summarized by the median of its 10 iterations (D1), so one slow iteration barely moves it; the geometric mean then averages medians.

    In depth: bootstrap intervals

    The idea, and the live widget where you can draw resamples from the real runs, is on How sure: the interval. Here is what the analysis does exactly: percentile bootstrap, 10 000 resamples, seed 0x5EED (24301), 95 % interval from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resampled statistics (Efron and Tibshirani 1993). No normality assumption.

    for b in 1..10000: draw 30 runs with replacement from the 30 (each group on its own) statistic_b = the statistic on that draw (a geometric mean, a ratio of them, ...) interval = [ percentile 2.5 of statistic_1..10000 , percentile 97.5 ]

    Why

    Why resample each group on its own? A speedup compares the N = 1 runs with the N = 8 runs; a RQ2 contrast compares four groups. The runs of different configurations are different processes, started at different times: there is no pairing between the 3rd run of one and the 3rd run of the other. So each group is resampled independently, and each bootstrap value is the statistic of the resampled groups (GM(resampled N = 1) / GM(resampled N = 8), say).

    A bootstrap p-value comes from the same 10 000 values: twice the share of them on the far side of "no effect" (1 for a ratio, 0 on the log scale), and never below 1/10 000, which is the resolution 10 000 resamples can give.

    In depth: the RQ2 metric

    How much does synchronization cost in one environment? Compare the atomic histogram with its lock-free control on the same input: r = ln T(atomic) - ln T(local). But r also holds things that are not synchronization, such as the dependency chain that slows the local control at k = 1. Those are the same in every environment at the same k, so compare r between environments (ADR 0026; with made-up numbers first on RQ2 with made-up numbers):

    Δr = r(browser) - r(native) exp(Δr) = [T_atomic / T_local in the browser] / [T_atomic / T_local natively] 1 = synchronization costs as much as natively; above 1 = more; below 1 = less

      In depth: the interaction on logs, and the RQ1 contrast

      Its interval resamples four groups of runs (browser atomic, browser local, native atomic, native local), each on its own. The RQ1 contrast is built the same way, with T(1) and T(N) in place of atomic and local: the browser's speedup over native's, an interaction of two ratios.

      In depth: Holm and the primary contrasts

      Why ten comparisons were named in advance and get a stricter bar is on Ten decisions, a stricter bar. The ten primary contrasts (plan decision D8): RQ2 at N = 8 for k = 1, 4, 16, 256 in both browsers (8), and RQ1 for the filter at N = 8 in both browsers (2). Everything else is exploratory: 95 % intervals, no decision.

      • Their intervals are Bonferroni 99.5 % intervals (1 - 0.05 / 10), so the ten hold together with 95 % confidence (family-wise error).
      • The decisions use Holm's step-down correction: sort the p-values, multiply the smallest by 10, the next by 9, and so on, keep a running maximum, and reject when the result is at most 0.05. It controls the same family error as Bonferroni and is never less powerful (Holm 1979).
      sort p(1) ≤ p(2) ≤ ... ≤ p(10) adjusted(i) = max over j ≤ i of min(1, (10 - j + 1) · p(j)) reject contrast i if adjusted(i) ≤ 0.05

      In depth: Mann-Whitney and Cliff's delta

      The bootstrap makes no normality assumption, but it is one method. As a robustness check, the plan repeats the 10 decisions with the course's default test for timing data: the Mann-Whitney U rank test, with Cliff's delta as its effect size.

      Why

      Why a second test at all? If two methods built on different assumptions reach the same ten decisions, the decisions do not hang on either method's assumptions. Mann-Whitney looks only at ranks (which of two runs was faster), never at how much, so it cannot be fooled by a few extreme values; the bootstrap uses the values themselves. Agreement between them is the point.

      Each run gives one ratio (log T1/TN for RQ1, log T atomic / T local for RQ2, the two cells paired by repetition index); the browser's 30 ratios are compared with native's, and the 10 p-values are Holm-adjusted.

      Cliff's δ = ( #{pairs where browser > native} - #{pairs where browser < native} ) / (30 · 30) +1 or -1 = complete separation; 0 = complete overlap

      In depth: Karp-Flatt

      Efficiency below 1 says that scaling is sub-linear, not why. Amdahl's law would blame a serial part: a fraction of the work that only one thread can do. The Karp-Flatt serial fraction (Karp and Flatt 1990) turns that around and asks which serial fraction would explain the measured speedup:

      e = (1/S - 1/N) / (1 - 1/N)

      A constant e across N reads as a serial part; an e that grows with N reads as an overhead that grows with the threads. For the filter (pick N):

        Why

        Why compute it, if the filter has no serial part? Because it is the standard first suspect, and the number shows it is the wrong one: the filter's timed region has no serial part, and in weak scaling T(N) does not stay flat either. So e here is a cost of running in parallel (shared memory bandwidth natively, a fixed per-iteration overhead in the browsers, neither measured directly), not a serial fraction of the code.         In summary. One value per run (the median of its 10 measured iterations), one time per configuration (the geometric mean of 30 runs), a percentile bootstrap interval over runs (10 000 resamples, fixed seed), Bonferroni intervals and Holm decisions for the ten primary contrasts, and Mann-Whitney as a second opinion. The analysis plan fixed all of it before the campaign (ADR 0037). Not rejecting means "could not tell", never "the same".
